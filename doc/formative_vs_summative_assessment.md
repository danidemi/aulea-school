# Formative vs. Summative Assessment: Core Principles

Assessment in education serves two distinct purposes that are often conflated. Understanding the difference — and deliberately using both — changes how feedback drives learning rather than just measuring it.

## The Core Distinction

### Formative Assessment
Assessment **for** learning. Its purpose is to generate feedback *during* the learning process, while there's still time to act on it. It's low-stakes (or no-stakes) by design — the point is information, not judgment.

Examples: short quizzes with immediate feedback, in-class polls, quick code reviews, "explain this back to me" checks, draft submissions with comments.

### Summative Assessment
Assessment **of** learning. Its purpose is to certify or measure achievement *after* a period of learning is complete. It's typically higher-stakes and produces a grade, pass/fail, or certification outcome.

Examples: final exams, certification tests, end-of-module projects graded for a final score.

**The key distinction is not the tool (a quiz can be either) — it's the *function*: is this feedback meant to be acted on, or is it a final verdict?**

---

## Why the Distinction Matters

A course built entirely on summative assessment gives learners no chance to correct course before the grade lands — errors are discovered too late to fix. A course built entirely on formative assessment measures nothing definitively and may produce no accountability or credential.

Effective courses use both, deliberately sequenced: **frequent formative checkpoints throughout, summative assessment at defined milestones.**

## Key Design Principles

| Principle | What It Means | Practical Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Feedback must be actionable** | Formative feedback that arrives too late or too vague is wasted | Give feedback while the learner can still revise or practice again |
| **Low stakes enable honesty** | Learners disengage or game low-value feedback if it "counts" too much | Keep formative checks ungraded or lightly weighted |
| **Frequency beats intensity** | Many small checks reveal gaps earlier than one big test | Prefer short, frequent quizzes over rare, long ones |
| **Self-assessment closes the loop** | External feedback alone doesn't build a learner's ability to judge their own understanding | Ask learners to predict their own score, or identify their weakest area, before revealing results |
| **Errors are data, not failure** | In formative contexts, a wrong answer is diagnostic information, not a penalty | Use common wrong answers as teaching material, not just "next question" |
| **Summative should reflect formative** | A final test should not surprise learners with content/format never seen in formative practice | Mirror the format and difficulty of formative checks in the final assessment |
| **Alignment with objectives** | Both types must measure what was actually taught, not tangential knowledge | Map each assessment item back to a specific learning objective |

## Implications for Teaching Technical Subjects

Technical and IT courses lend themselves particularly well to formative assessment because outcomes are often immediately verifiable: code either compiles/runs correctly or it doesn't, a configuration either works or fails. This gives near-instant, unambiguous feedback — a resource many other subjects lack.

Practical patterns for technical courses:
- **Frequent short quizzes** on discrete topics (syntax, concepts, patterns) with immediate correct/incorrect + explanation
- **Debugging exercises** as formative tools — the "bug" is a deliberate diagnostic, not a punishment
- **Common-error galleries**: collect recurring mistakes across a cohort and turn them into shared teaching cases
- **Milestone projects** as summative checkpoints, each preceded by formative practice on the exact skills the project will require

## One-Line Summary

Formative assessment is feedback learners can still act on; summative assessment is the final measure of what they did with it — a well-designed course never lets the second replace the first.
