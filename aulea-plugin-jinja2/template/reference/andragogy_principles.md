# Andragogy: Core Principles for Teaching Adults

Andragogy, formalized by Malcolm Knowles, describes how adult learning differs from child learning (pedagogy). It rests on six assumptions about the adult learner.

## 1. Need to Know

Adults need to understand **why** they are learning something before they engage with it. Unlike children, who often accept instruction on authority, adults disengage from content whose purpose isn't clear. Every unit should open with the practical payoff, not the theory.

## 2. Self-Concept of the Learner

Adults see themselves as autonomous and self-directed, not as passive recipients of instruction. They resist being "talked at" and expect a voice in what and how they learn. Effective teaching involves them in decisions — pacing, project choice, sequencing — rather than dictating a fixed path.

## 3. Prior Experience

Adults arrive with a reservoir of experience that is a resource, not a blank slate to overwrite. This experience creates individual differences that don't exist in a room of children the same age. Teaching should connect new material to what learners already know, and use peer experience as a teaching resource (discussion, peer instruction) rather than ignoring it.

## 4. Readiness to Learn

Adults become ready to learn when they encounter a real need — a job requirement, a gap blocking their progress, a life transition. Motivation is strongest when content maps onto a problem the learner already recognizes as theirs. Timing and framing matter as much as content quality.

## 5. Orientation to Learning

Adults are problem-centered rather than subject-centered. They learn best when content is organized around real tasks to be solved, not abstract topics to be covered. "Let's fix this bug" motivates more than "today we study pointers in the abstract."

## 6. Motivation to Learn

Adults respond more to internal motivators (growth, competence, self-esteem, curiosity) than external ones (grades, threats, rewards). External pressure can still play a role, but sustainable engagement comes from a sense of progress and relevance to personal or professional goals.

---

## Practical Implications for Course Design

| Principle | Design Response |
|---|---|
| Need to know | Open every module with relevance: "what problem this solves" |
| Self-concept | Offer choices in exercises, pacing, or project direction |
| Prior experience | Elicit and build on what learners already know; use peer teaching |
| Readiness | Sequence topics to match real, felt needs — not just a logical syllabus order |
| Problem orientation | Frame lessons around tasks/problems, not topics |
| Internal motivation | Emphasize competence and visible progress over grades |

## One-Line Summary

Adults learn best when they understand *why*, retain control over *how*, and see content anchored in *real problems* that connect to what they already know and want to achieve.
